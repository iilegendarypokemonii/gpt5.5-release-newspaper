Your ChatGPT just got rewired

GPT-5.5 is in your account this week — OpenAI's first ground-up retrain in over a year, with new tiers, twice the memory, and an agent that now finishes overnight jobs.

By the automated research deskReported across 51 public-source passesFiled Apr 25

Smarter at answering. Twice as confident when it shouldn't be.

If you pay for ChatGPT, you got a new model this week. The brain answering your prompts is now GPT-5.5, the first ground-up retrain OpenAI has shipped since February 2025. Most of your daily use will feel similar. The differences are the news.

The upgrade over GPT-5.4 is the real story. The same job now finishes with about 40% fewer output tokens, on roughly twice the context. Independent benchmarks line up: Databricks measured a 46% error reduction on document-reasoning, CodeRabbit's bug-detection rate jumped from 58% to 79%, and XBOW now misses only 10% of known vulnerabilities — down from 40% on GPT-5 and 18% on Opus 4.6. The biggest jump in over a year, and the first OpenAI release in fourteen months that feels like a generation step rather than another fine-tune of the same base.

Three things will catch you out day-to-day. Replies run longer than before, so ask for short if you want short. Vague prompts get treated as plans — the model takes intent literally and runs with it, so tighten what you write. And on the developer side, Codex now finishes the kind of overnight runs that quietly died on the previous version.

And one bonus from the same week: ChatGPT's image generation took a real step up. The new gpt-image-2 model finally handles dense, text-heavy illustrations cleanly — diagrams, infographics, posters with legible labels — the longstanding weak spot of model-generated images.

Read on for the working translation: what is real, what is hype, and the one thing GPT-5.5 got noticeably worse at.

Reliability desk

The honesty gap

Sharper at answering. Worse at saying it does not know. Artificial Analysis ran the same uncertainty test across the three top models: how often does it invent an answer when it does not actually have one?

GPT-5.5
86%
Gemini 3.1 Pro
50%
Opus 4.7
36%

Higher = more confident bluffing. Source: Artificial Analysis Omniscience. For medical, legal, financial, or client-facing work, ask the model to flag what it is not sure about and verify the result.

GPT-5.5 also leads the field on raw accuracy in the same test, at 57%. Artificial Analysis's own framing of the score gain: it was "largely driven by knowledge, with a modest improvement in hallucination." The model knows more facts than its competitors. It did not get better at recognising when it doesn't know one.

The story is contested. OpenAI's launch slides claim a 60% hallucination drop on internal benchmarks, and Bank of New York's CIO, using GPT-5.5 in regulated-finance testing, reported "impressive hallucination resistance." Omniscience is the only third-party measurement of the specific behaviour — answering when the model should not — and is the rate that has held up under outside scrutiny.

Price desk

Top of the cost curve

OpenAI doubled the sticker. Input rose from $2.50 to $5 per million tokens; output from $15 to $30 — above Opus 4.7's $5/$25, and roughly a hundred times the price of DeepSeek's open-weights V4 Flash, which shipped the same day. Decrypt's launch-day verdict has become consensus: Faster, smarter — and pricier.

GPT-5.5 uses about 40% fewer output tokens to finish the same task, so the real bill is closer to +20% versus GPT-5.4 — not the +100% the sticker suggests. Benchmark on your own workload before standardising.

Reasoning tier shifts the picture again. GPT-5.5 supports low, medium, high, and extra-high effort, and now defaults to medium where GPT-5.4 defaulted to none. The swing across the range is large: Simon Willison ran his standing benchmark — "Generate an SVG of a pelican riding a bicycle" — at default and again at extra-high, and watched reasoning tokens climb from 39 to 9,322 on the same call. About 240× the per-call cost for the same prompt. Heavy testers recommend setting reasoning_effort: low for routine work and raising the level only when a task is genuinely hard.

What to do Monday

Practical playbook

Pick the right model

If the work isLong codingGPT-5.5, especially in Codex. Persistence, tools, and overnight runs are where it leads.
If the work isResearchTry Gemini 3.1 Pro first. It still wins three of the ten Artificial Analysis evals at lower cost.
If the work isFrontend designOpus 4.7 leads on visual and design-heavy UI. The dev heuristic: Opus for design, GPT-5.5 for logic.
If the work isBudget scaleDeepSeek V4-Flash at $0.14 / $0.28 per million. Best where context is large and cost dominates.

Tighten how you ask

  • Ask for uncertainty explicitly on medical, legal, financial, regulated, or client-facing work. Treat confident detail as a thing to verify.
  • Default to lower reasoning effort for ordinary tasks. GPT-5.5 reasons by default; that costs tokens and time on prompts that do not need it.
  • Say what to change and what not to. The model takes you literally and treats vague intent as a plan. Specify scope and length.
  • Benchmark before standardising on your own prompts, documents, and acceptance criteria. The size of the gain varies wildly by task.

Building an app? Mix the models.

One specific weakness identified in launch week: on polished frontend work — UI components, visual layouts, design-heavy CSS — Opus 4.7 still leads. GPT-5.5's strength is logic, persistence, and long autonomous runs. A single-model app workflow leaves quality on the table.

The fix. Route the project by stage rather than committing to one model:

  • Mockups, screens, labelled infographics → gpt-image-2. The new image model finally handles dense, text-heavy layouts the previous one butchered.
  • Frontend code, CSS, design polish → Opus 4.7. Launch reviewers consistently picked it over GPT-5.5 here.
  • Backend logic, agentic flows, long Codex runs → GPT-5.5. Persistence and tool use are where it leads.
